Effect of Baxdrostat on Ambulatory Blood Pressure in Patients with Resistant Hypertension (Bax24)

Azizi M, Brown JM, Dwyer JP, Flack JM, Jones ESW, Kurlyandskaya R, Li H, Birve F, Lihn AS, Perl S, Schlaich MP, Shibata H, Wang J-G, Williams B, for the Bax24 investigators.
Lancet 2026;407:988-99 (7 March 2026) · Phase 3, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial · ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06168409 · Funder: AstraZeneca · Open access (CC BY 4.0)
Source file: Baxdrostat Lancet 2026.pdf (Disease/Blood Pressure; the file baxdrostat.pdf in the same folder is an identical copy of this paper)

At a glance

  • Design: Phase 3, international (79 sites, 22 countries), randomised 1:1, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 2-week placebo run-in, 12-week treatment, 2-week safety follow-up.
  • Population: 217 treated adults with true resistant hypertension (seated SBP 140 to <170 mm Hg on ≥3 drugs incl. a diuretic) and confirmed 24-h ambulatory SBP ≥130 mm Hg.
  • Intervention: Baxdrostat 2 mg once daily (n=108) vs placebo (n=109), added to background therapy.
  • Primary result: change in 24-h ambulatory SBP at week 12: –16.6 mm Hg (baxdrostat) vs –2.6 (placebo); placebo-corrected –14.0 mm Hg (95% CI –17.2 to –10.8; P<0.0001).
  • Main risks: hyperkalaemia (K+ >6 mmol/L in 5% centrally, 3% confirmed vs 0%), eGFR fall (>30% in 24% vs 5%), AEs overall 52% vs 37%.

1. Patient population studied

Eligibility (p.3)

  • Adults aged ≥18 years with resistant hypertension: mean seated office SBP ≥140 and <170 mm Hg despite maximally tolerated doses of ≥3 antihypertensive drugs of different classes, including a diuretic, for ≥4 weeks.
  • eGFR ≥45 ml/min/1.73 m²; serum potassium ≥3.5 and <5.0 mmol/L.
  • After the 2-week placebo run-in: mean 24-h ambulatory SBP ≥130 mm Hg (measured after witnessed intake of background drugs) and ≥80% adherence to placebo (pill count) – i.e. resistance was confirmed by ABPM to exclude white-coat effect and non-adherence.
  • Exclusions (main article): SBP ≥170 mm Hg; MRA or potassium-sparing diuretic within 4 weeks of screening and during the study; HbA1c >9.5%; secondary hypertension (except sleep apnoea or primary aldosteronism – so primary aldosteronism was not excluded); cardiovascular/cerebrovascular event within 6 months; persistent atrial fibrillation. Full criteria are in the appendix.

2. Intervention treatment protocol

  • Baxdrostat 2 mg orally once daily (fixed dose; no titration, no dose reduction permitted) for 12 weeks, added to unchanged background antihypertensive therapy (p.3).
  • Sequence: 2-week placebo run-in → 12-week double-blind treatment → 2-week post-treatment safety follow-up (week 14).
  • Randomisation: 1:1 via interactive response technology; computer-generated codes from an independent vendor; fixed block size of four; stratified by baseline ambulatory SBP (<140 vs ≥140 mm Hg). Baxdrostat and placebo tablets identical in appearance and labelling; allocation concealed from investigators, patients and staff.
  • Background therapy: maintained throughout; rescue medication only if seated SBP >170 or DBP >105 mm Hg.
  • Hyperkalaemia: temporary or permanent discontinuation allowed (detailed rules in the appendix).
  • Monitoring: safety labs (K+, Na+, creatinine) at weeks 1 and 2 without study-drug administration at the site; central lab (blinded) for K+, Na+, creatinine, aldosterone, plasma renin activity, baxdrostat levels, plus local-lab K+.
  • BP measurement: seated office BP (Microlife WatchBP Office 2G) at trough before dosing; 24-h ABPM (Microlife WatchBP O3; ≥25 h recording, ≥24 h analysed; daytime 06:00–21:59 every 20 min, night-time 22:00–05:59 every 30 min; valid if ≥20 h, ≥70% of readings, no gap >2 h) at baseline and week 12 after witnessed intake of background drugs and study drug.

3. Control group treatment protocol

  • Matching placebo once daily for 12 weeks after the same 2-week placebo run-in, plus the same unchanged background therapy (p.3).
  • Identical masking, visit schedule, laboratory surveillance, BP/ABPM methods, rescue thresholds and 2-week follow-up as in the baxdrostat group.
  • Rescue antihypertensives were used by 3 placebo patients (3%) and by none in the baxdrostat group (p.6).

Other findings

  • Individual response: all but 4 baxdrostat-treated patients showed a fall in 24-h SBP; the reduction was seen across the whole 24 h, day and night (hourly profile, Figure 2).
  • Early/late office BP: seated SBP/DBP were lower than with placebo already at weeks 4 and 8 (details in appendix). At week 14 (2 weeks after stopping), seated BP was 137.7/80.5 mm Hg (baxdrostat, n=99) vs 142.6/84.1 mm Hg (placebo, n=105).
  • Hormones: median aldosterone change –5.1 ng/dL (≈ –76%) vs –0.4 (≈ +2%); median plasma renin activity change +1.9 vs +0.3 ng/mL/h; aldosterone/renin ratio –3.5 vs –0.8.
  • Drug exposure: baxdrostat below the quantification limit in 11% (week 4) and 15% (week 12) of tested patients.

Key safety observations

  • Most common AEs: hyperkalaemia, headache and hypotension (individual counts in the appendix). No unexpected AEs, no deaths and no cases of adrenal insufficiency were reported.
  • Potassium: rises occurred mainly in the first 2 weeks, were stable thereafter and returned toward baseline within 2 weeks of stopping. Conversely, hypokalaemia was less common than with placebo.
  • Sodium: changes mainly in the first 2 weeks, returning toward baseline after treatment stopped.
  • Kidney function: mean eGFR change at week 12: –8.7 ± 14.0 (baxdrostat) vs –0.4 ± 9.7 ml/min/1.73 m² (placebo). The fall occurred mainly in weeks 2–4 and returned toward baseline by week 14 – interpreted as a reversible haemodynamic effect. (Table 3 says "more than 30%", the text "30% or more" – same counts.)
  • Not reported in the main article: cortisol/ACTH findings, sexual/endocrine AEs, albuminuria, numbers of temporary drug interruptions.

6. Discussion of the study

Authors' interpretation

  • Baxdrostat 2 mg lowered 24-h ambulatory SBP by 14.0 mm Hg beyond placebo in true resistant hypertension; effects were similar by day (–14.1) and night (–13.9), consistent across prespecified subgroups, and 71% vs 17% reached 24-h SBP <130 mm Hg (~4-fold).
  • The authors state these are the largest ambulatory SBP reductions reported in a randomised trial of a therapeutic intervention in true resistant hypertension (author claim; not independently verified here). Ambulatory reduction exceeded office reduction (–14.0 vs –10.3), unlike most antihypertensive trials – possibly due to less white-coat effect, ABPM after observed dosing vs trough office BP, and the strong nocturnal effect.
  • Mechanistic reading: ≈76% aldosterone reduction and rising renin are consistent with enzyme inhibition and increased sodium excretion in a sodium-retaining state; the ≈30-h half-life may support the sustained night-time effect. Raised potassium and the reversible eGFR fall are viewed as expected consequences of aldosterone-pathway inhibition (similar to spironolactone in PATHWAY-2: eGFR –9.7 at 12 weeks) and also seen early with finerenone or SGLT2 inhibitors.
  • Bax24 independently confirms the BaxHTN findings in an ambulatory-confirmed, resistant-only population. Compared with lorundrostat (Advance-HTN: –7.9 mm Hg 24-h SBP at 50 mg), the effect was numerically larger, but cross-trial comparisons are limited by differences in populations and placebo response (placebo –2.6 mm Hg here).
  • Elevated ambulatory BP (especially at night) is linked to mortality in observational data (≈60,000 patients) – but the authors emphasise that outcome trials are needed to show that baxdrostat improves cardiovascular and renal outcomes (ongoing: BaxHTN/BaxAsia long-term studies, and baxdrostat–dapagliflozin trials in CKD and heart failure).
  • Limitations named by the authors: women and Black patients under-represented; primary analysis restricted to patients with valid ABPM (89/95 of 217); no chemical (blood/urine) adherence testing for background drugs; only 12 weeks of treatment; low severe-hyperkalaemia rates seen in populations with largely preserved kidney function and need monitoring soon after initiation, like MRAs.

Critical appraisal (reviewer's perspective, based on the article)

  • Strengths: ABPM as primary endpoint (objective, little placebo response); stringent confirmation of true resistance (run-in, observed dosing, pill counts, ABPM ≥130 mm Hg); double-blind with concealed allocation; hierarchical testing; consistent effect in day, night, office and DBP endpoints; baxdrostat drug levels and hormone data support biological plausibility and adherence.
  • Magnitude vs reality: the effect is large, but the trial is small (217 treated), short (12 weeks), and the primary analysis includes only 184 patients (85% of those treated; 19 baxdrostat vs 14 placebo patients missing/invalid), so a modest risk of bias from non-random loss exists, partly mitigated by the sensitivity analysis. The estimate is probably a best-case scenario for a selected, adherent, monitored trial population.
  • Safety is not trivial: AEs 52% vs 37%; 5% discontinued for AEs vs 0%; K+ >5.5 mmol/L in 12% and >6.0 in 5% (3% confirmed); and about 1 in 4 patients had an eGFR drop of more than 30%. Patients with eGFR <45, K+ ≥5.0, or on MRAs were excluded, so these rates likely underestimate real-world risk in CKD or diabetic patients – eGFR <60 and age ≥75 subgroups were too small to analyse.
  • No active comparator: no head-to-head comparison with spironolactone/eplerenone, so whether baxdrostat is more effective or better tolerated than the current fourth-line standard is unknown.
  • Sponsor involvement: AstraZeneca participated in design, data collection, analysis, interpretation and writing; several authors are employees/shareholders; the last author (B Williams) chaired the AstraZeneca executive committee for the baxdrostat hypertension trials; medical writing was sponsor-funded. Most safety details sit in the appendix.
  • Take-home: in patients with confirmed resistant hypertension, baxdrostat 2 mg produced a clinically important and 24-hour-sustained BP reduction, with manageable but frequent electrolyte/renal changes that call for early laboratory monitoring. Long-term outcome data and head-to-head comparison with MRAs are the key missing pieces.
